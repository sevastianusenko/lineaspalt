Every spring, the same scene plays out on Lancaster County driveways and parking lots. A pothole opens up after a hard winter. Someone buys a few bags of cold patch, shovels it in, drives over it a few times, and the hole disappears. By the next spring, the hole is back, often a little bigger.

That is not because cold patch is a bad product. It is because cold patch is built for a different job than most people use it for. This guide explains the difference between cold patch and hot mix asphalt, when each one belongs, and what a patch has to look like if you want it to outlast the next winter.

![Freshly laid asphalt driveway edge with gravel shoulder and caution tape](/img/driveway-resurfaced-edge-gravel.webp)

## What cold patch asphalt is

Cold patch is an asphalt mix designed to stay workable at outdoor temperatures. Instead of being heated at a plant and delivered hot, it uses a binder that keeps the stone loose and sticky in the bag or the stockpile. You can open it in January, scoop it into a hole, and compact it with a tamper or a tire.

That convenience comes with trade-offs:

- **It never fully hardens like hot mix.** The binder cures slowly, and some products stay soft for a long time.
- **It does not bond strongly to the old asphalt.** It sits in the hole more than it becomes part of the pavement.
- **It is often placed in one thick lump.** Without proper compaction, it stays porous, and water passes straight through it.

Cold patch is a stopgap. It is very good at what it is meant for: making a dangerous hole safe right now, in weather when nothing else is available.

## What hot mix asphalt is

Hot mix is the same material roads and driveways are paved with. Stone and asphalt binder are heated together at a plant and delivered hot, typically at well over 250 degrees Fahrenheit. While it is hot, it can be spread, shaped and compacted into a dense, solid layer that bonds to the surrounding pavement. As it cools, it hardens into real pavement.

Hot mix is what permanent asphalt repair is made from. The catch is availability. Asphalt plants in our area generally shut down or cut back in the coldest months, and hot mix loses its workability quickly once it cools. That is why the season for permanent patching is roughly spring through late fall.

## Cold patch vs hot mix at a glance

| | Cold patch | Hot mix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Temporary, emergency repair | Permanent repair |
| When it can be used | Any season, even in cold weather | Spring to late fall, when plants run and the ground is not frozen |
| Bond to old asphalt | Weak | Strong, especially with a tack coat |
| Density after compaction | Lower, often porous | High, close to the original pavement |
| Typical life | A season or less in traffic | Years, when the base is sound |
| Who can apply it | Anyone with a shovel and a tamper | A crew with equipment and a supply of hot asphalt |

## When cold patching makes sense

We use cold patch ourselves. It is the right tool when:

- **It is winter** and a hole in a driveway or a lot is a real hazard to tires, ankles or snow plows.
- **The repair has to happen today** and a permanent fix is not possible until the plants are running.
- **The area is going to be rebuilt soon anyway,** and you only need to get through a few weeks.
- **The hole is small and shallow** in a low traffic spot, and you accept that it is temporary.

The mistake is treating a cold patch as the finished repair. A good rule: every cold patch should have a follow up date for a permanent repair written down somewhere.

## Why cold patches fail

Cold patches usually fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the bag and everything to do with the hole.

1. **Water was still in the hole.** Patch placed in standing water or on a wet, soft base never bonds and gets pushed out by the next freeze.
2. **The edges were never cut back.** A pothole is surrounded by weak, cracked asphalt. A patch that sits against crumbling edges loses its grip as those edges break away.
3. **The base under the hole had failed.** Potholes start below the surface. If the stone base is soft or washed out, no surface patch will hold.
4. **It was not compacted.** Loose patch is porous. Water runs through it, freezes, and lifts it out.
5. **It was mounded on top.** Overfilling leaves a hump that tires and plow blades hit, tearing the patch out.

Hot mix fails for the same reasons when it is placed carelessly. The material matters, but the preparation decides whether the repair lasts.

## How a permanent asphalt patch is built

A patch that becomes part of the pavement goes in like this.

### 1. Cut back to sound asphalt

The damaged area is marked out as a square or rectangle that extends past the visible hole into solid pavement. The edges are cut vertically with a saw or similar tool. Square, vertical edges give the new asphalt something solid to press against and bond to.

### 2. Remove the failed material

Everything loose inside the cut comes out: broken asphalt, mud, water and soft base material.

### 3. Repair the base

If the stone base is soft, wet or washed out, it is dug out and replaced with compacted stone. This is the step that decides whether the pothole comes back. A surface patch over a failed base is temporary no matter what it is made of.

### 4. Tack coat

A thin layer of asphalt emulsion is applied to the vertical edges and the bottom of the hole. It works like glue between the old pavement and the new hot mix.

### 5. Place hot mix in lifts

Hot mix goes in in layers, often two inches or so at a time for deeper holes, and each layer is compacted before the next one goes on. One thick pour cannot be compacted all the way through.

### 6. Compact and finish flush

The final lift is compacted until it is level with the surrounding surface. Not mounded, not dipped.

### 7. Seal the seam

The joint where new asphalt meets old is sealed so water cannot work its way down the edge of the patch.

## Patch, repair or replace?

Patching works when the damage is local. A few holes in an otherwise solid driveway or lot are good candidates. Patching stops making sense when:

- Alligator cracking covers a large part of the surface.
- Potholes keep returning in the same spots even after proper repairs, which points to a drainage or base problem.
- The surface is crumbling at the edges along most of the driveway.
- Large areas have sunk or heaved.

At that point, larger repairs or an overlay are worth discussing, and an honest contractor will tell you so. Our guide to [pothole repair vs. full repaving](/pothole-repair-vs-full-repaving-which-one-do-you-actually-need/) goes through how to decide.

## If you are patching it yourself

Homeowners can do a reasonable temporary cold patch. To give it the best chance:

1. **Pick a dry day.** Sweep and blow out the hole. Get the water out.
2. **Remove loose pieces** and anything crumbling at the edges.
3. **Fill in layers,** an inch or two at a time, compacting each layer hard with a hand tamper. A car tire driven slowly over a board on top of the patch works for the final pass.
4. **Finish slightly above grade,** so it compacts down level under traffic, but do not leave a mound.
5. **Keep it as a stopgap.** Plan a permanent repair when the season allows.

What a homeowner usually cannot do is the base repair and the hot mix. That is where a crew earns its keep.

## How to tell a good patch from your porch

- The patch has **straight edges** and a square or rectangular shape, not an irregular blob.
- It is **level** with the surrounding surface.
- The **seam is tight,** with no gap where water can enter.
- It **feels solid** underfoot, not spongy.
- A year later, it **still looks the same.**

If you see a round lump of loose asphalt sitting proud of the surface, you are looking at a cold patch. Fine for February. Not fine as the final repair.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does cold patch last?

It depends on the hole, the weather and the traffic. In a dry, well prepared hole in a low traffic area, a cold patch can hold for a season or more. In a wet hole in a busy lot, it may last weeks. Treat it as temporary.

### Can hot mix be used in winter?

Rarely. Most plants in our area cut back or close in the coldest months, and hot mix cools too fast to work properly in freezing weather. Permanent patching is a spring through fall job.

### Is cold patch good enough for a driveway?

For a temporary fix, yes. For a permanent repair, no. Plan a hot mix repair when the season allows.

### Why do you cut the hole bigger?

Because the asphalt around a pothole is already weakened. Cutting back to sound pavement removes the weak edge and gives the patch a solid wall to bond to.

### Should I seal the driveway after patching?

Often, yes. Once patches have cured, a sealcoat makes the surface uniform and protects the pavement around the repairs. Read [driveway sealing vs sealcoating](/driveway-sealing-vs-sealcoating/) for what that involves.
