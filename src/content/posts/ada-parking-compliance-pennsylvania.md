## Who Has to Comply (Short Answer: Almost Everyone)

The ADA applies to places of “public accommodation”, retail stores, restaurants, offices, medical practices, churches with public programs, hotels, and just about any business customers visit. It’s federal law, so it applies in Pennsylvania regardless of what your township requires, and it applies to existing lots, not just new construction. There is no grandfather clause for parking. If your lot serves the public, the requirements below are your requirements.

The two authoritative sources, if you want to read the rules yourself, are [ADA.gov](https://www.ada.gov/) and the [U.S. Access Board](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/), which publishes the technical standards every measurement in this article comes from.

## How Many Accessible Spaces Your Lot Needs

The count is based on the total number of parking spaces in your lot. The baseline ratio is one accessible space per 25 total spaces, and the full table looks like this:

Two important add-ons. First: at least one of every six accessible spaces (or fraction thereof) must be **van-accessible**, so even a lot with a single accessible space needs that space to be van-accessible. Second: medical offices have stricter ratios. Outpatient facilities need 10 percent of patient parking accessible; rehabilitation and outpatient physical therapy facilities need 20 percent.

## The Exact Dimensions: Spaces and Access Aisles

![Blue ADA symbol and hatching on a lot lit by street lights](/img/night-lot-blue-ada-hatching.webp)
*Two accessible spaces sharing one crosshatched access aisle, permitted by the standards, and an efficient layout.*

This is where most lots fail, because the numbers are specific and eyeballing doesn’t cut it:

-   **Standard accessible space:** at least 96 inches (8 feet) wide, with an access aisle at least **60 inches** (5 feet) wide alongside it.
-   **Van-accessible space, option one:** a 132-inch (11-foot) wide space with a 60-inch access aisle.
-   **Van-accessible space, option two:** a 96-inch space with a **96-inch** (8-foot) access aisle, the extra width accommodates a wheelchair lift deploying from the side of a van.
-   **Access aisles** must be marked to discourage parking in them (crosshatching is the standard method), must be level, and must connect to an accessible route to the building entrance, not force someone to travel behind parked cars.
-   **Two spaces may share one access aisle.** That’s a legitimate, space-efficient layout we use often.

One more detail that surprises owners: slope. Accessible spaces and their aisles can’t exceed a 1:48 slope (about 2 percent) in any direction. A space painted on a noticeable grade is non-compliant no matter how perfect the striping is, because a wheelchair user can’t safely transfer on a slope.

## Signs: The Requirement Paint Can’t Satisfy

The painted symbol on the pavement is not enough by itself. Each accessible space must have a vertical sign displaying the International Symbol of Accessibility, mounted so the bottom of the sign is at least **60 inches above the ground**, high enough that a parked vehicle can’t hide it. Van-accessible spaces need an additional “van accessible” designation on the sign. The one exception: lots with four or fewer total spaces must still provide an accessible space, but the sign is not required.

Why the sign requirement matters so much: pavement markings disappear under snow. In a Pennsylvania January, the sign is the only thing marking that space, which is exactly why enforcement treats missing signage as a serious violation, not a technicality.

## Location: Closest Spaces to the Accessible Entrance

![ADA parking stall with white outline and access aisle hatching beside a historic building](/img/ada-stall-white-outline-brick-building.webp)
*Accessible parking positioned on the shortest route to the entrance, placement is part of the requirement.*

Accessible spaces must be located on the shortest accessible route to the building’s accessible entrance. You can’t tuck them at the far end of the lot because the geometry was easier there. If your building has multiple entrances, the spaces should be distributed to serve them. And the route from the access aisle to the door must be genuinely accessible, curb ramps where needed, no stairs, no gravel gaps. We flag route problems during layout because a perfectly striped space that dead-ends at a curb still fails.

## What Non-Compliance Actually Costs in Pennsylvania

ADA parking violations are enforced two ways. The Department of Justice can pursue civil penalties, currently over $75,000 for a first violation and over $150,000 for subsequent ones. Far more common in Pennsylvania, though, are private lawsuits and demand letters. Serial plaintiffs check lots for exactly the items in this guide: missing van-accessible spaces, faded or absent aisle markings, low or missing signs. Settlements routinely run into the thousands, plus attorney fees, plus the cost of the fix you needed anyway.

## Compliance Is Ongoing, Not One-Time

A compliant lot can drift out of compliance without anyone touching it. Pennsylvania winters are the main culprit: plow blades scrape pavement markings, and two or three seasons of plowing can erase a crosshatched access aisle almost completely. A space with a faded symbol and an invisible aisle is treated as non-compliant, regardless of what it looked like when it was painted. Signs get hit by trucks, bent, or removed during other site work and never reinstalled.

The practical answer is to fold an accessibility check into your regular restriping cycle, for most commercial lots, every two to three years. It adds nothing to the mobilization cost, and it means your compliance documentation stays current. We photograph accessible spaces, aisles, and signage on every job, so if a claim ever does arrive, you have dated evidence that the lot met the standards.

Snow management belongs in this conversation too. A legally perfect accessible space that spends January buried under the plow pile is a barrier, and complaints about snow-blocked accessible parking are common. Tell your plow contractor where the snow can’t go; it’s a one-sentence instruction that prevents a real problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### My lot was striped years ago. Am I grandfathered in?

No. The ADA requires barrier removal in existing facilities where it is “readily achievable,” and restriping a parking lot is the textbook example of readily achievable. If your lot predates the 2010 Standards, you’re expected to bring it up to current requirements the next time the lot is restriped, and plaintiffs’ attorneys know that. Age of the lot is not a defense; if anything, an old lot that has been repainted since 2012 without being corrected is a stronger case for the plaintiff, because the owner had the opportunity to comply and didn’t.

### Does Pennsylvania add requirements beyond the federal ADA?

Pennsylvania enforces accessibility through its Uniform Construction Code, which incorporates accessibility standards closely aligned with the federal rules, and local codes can add signage details. The federal ADA numbers in this guide are your floor everywhere in the state. When local rules differ, the stricter requirement wins, we check both during layout.

### What color does an accessible space have to be?

The federal standards actually don’t mandate blue paint, they require the spaces and aisles to be marked and the vertical sign to be present. Blue with the white symbol is the universal convention and what inspectors and the public expect, so that’s what we install. The crosshatched aisle marking, however, is effectively required, since aisles must be marked to discourage parking.

### Can I lose spaces by adding accessible parking?

Usually one or two, sometimes none. Because two accessible spaces can share a single access aisle, a thoughtful layout often converts three standard stalls into two compliant accessible spaces. On most lots we can rework the row nearest the entrance with minimal loss, that’s a layout problem, and layout problems are solvable.
