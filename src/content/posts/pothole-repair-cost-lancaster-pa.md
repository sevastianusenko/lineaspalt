## Why There’s a $400 Minimum (It’s Not Greed, It’s Physics)

A proper pothole repair isn’t a guy with a shovel and a bag from the hardware store. It’s a crew, a truck, hot asphalt that has to be bought fresh from a plant and kept hot, a saw or grinder for cutting clean edges, and a compactor. Hot mix asphalt doesn’t come in convenient single servings, plants sell it by the ton, it starts cooling the moment it’s loaded, and once it drops below working temperature it’s a very expensive doorstop.

So whether we’re fixing one pothole or nine, the cost of showing up is nearly identical: same crew, same equipment, same material minimum, same fuel. That fixed cost is the service call. Any contractor quoting dramatically less is either cutting a corner you’ll rediscover next spring, or planning to “find” extra problems once they’re in your driveway. ## Cold Patch vs. Hot Mix: The $50 Fix vs. the Real Fix

This is the part of the estimate where prices split into two universes, so it’s worth two minutes of your time.

**Cold patch** is the bagged asphalt you can buy at any home center. It never really cures, it just gets compressed by traffic and hopes for the best. It has a legitimate job: it’s a winter Band-Aid, something to keep a hole from eating tires until real repairs are possible. Highway departments use it in January for exactly that reason. The [Federal Highway Administration](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/) has studied pothole patching extensively, and the short version is: temporary materials give temporary results.

**Hot mix asphalt** arrives at 300°F, gets placed in a properly prepared hole, and is compacted in layers until it’s as dense as the pavement around it. Done right, a hot mix patch isn’t a patch so much as a small piece of brand-new pavement. That’s the difference between a repair that lasts a season and one that lasts a decade, and it’s why the price difference exists. The [Asphalt Institute](https://www.asphaltinstitute.org/) publishes the technical standards behind all of this if you enjoy bedtime reading about aggregate gradation.

## What Actually Drives the Price Up (or Down)

Two potholes that look identical from your porch can cost very different amounts to fix. Here’s what we’re looking at when we quote:

-   **Depth and base damage.** A shallow surface hole is quick. A hole where water has been churning the stone base into soup needs excavation and fresh base material before any asphalt goes in. The base is most of the labor.
-   **Number of holes.** The second, third, and fourth potholes are much cheaper than the first, because the crew and equipment are already there. This is why bundling repairs saves real money.
-   **Edges and cutting.** Ragged, crumbling edges need to be square-cut back to solid asphalt. More crumble, more cutting, more material.
-   **Access.** A driveway the truck can back right up to is easy. A hole behind a fence, under low wires, or at the bottom of a steep shared lane takes longer.
-   **Season.** Asphalt plants in our area generally run from spring through late fall. Winter emergency work means cold patch, at cold-patch prices, with a plan to come back and do it right.

## Why the “$50 Pothole Guy” Ends Up Costing You More

Every spring, someone knocks on doors in Lancaster County offering to fix potholes for pocket change. Here’s what that service usually looks like: shovel cold patch into the hole, dirt, water, loose gravel and all, stomp it a few times, collect cash, drive away. In the trade this is called “throw-and-go,” and it has roughly the lifespan of a snowman.

Without clean, square-cut edges, the patch has nothing solid to bond to. Without a compacted base, it settles. Without layered compaction, it’s porous, and water walks right through it. One freeze-thaw season later, the patch heaves out in chunks and your pothole is back, now bigger, because the failed patch tore away more of the surrounding asphalt on its way out. You’ve paid $50 to make a $400 problem into a $600 one. Do that twice and you’ve spent real repair money on repairs that didn’t happen.

## Patch It or Repave It? The $800 Question

There’s a point where patching stops making sense. If more than a quarter of your driveway is potholes, alligator cracking, and previous patches, you’re not maintaining pavement anymore, you’re doing very slow, very expensive repaving one square foot at a time. A good rule of thumb: when the cost of this year’s patches plus next year’s inevitable patches approaches half the cost of resurfacing, resurface.

We put together a full decision guide on this, [pothole repair vs. full repaving](/pothole-repair-vs-full-repaving-which-one-do-you-actually-need/), that walks through the math with real scenarios. Short version: one to a handful of holes in otherwise solid asphalt, patch with confidence. Pavement that’s failing everywhere at once, stop feeding it patches.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to fix one pothole?

With our $400 minimum service call, one properly repaired pothole runs $400, and that same visit can usually handle two or three more holes for modest additional material cost. That’s why we always suggest walking your whole driveway before we come out: the marginal cost of “while you’re here” is the best deal in asphalt.

### Can’t I just fill it myself with a bag of cold patch?

You can, and for a winter stopgap you absolutely should, it protects your tires and keeps the hole from growing as fast. Just know what you’re buying: a temporary plug, not a repair. Most DIY cold patch washes out or heaves out within a season because there’s no edge prep, no base repair, and no real compaction under it.

### How long does a professional pothole repair last?

A hot mix patch with square-cut edges, repaired base, and layered compaction should last 5–10 years or more, often as long as the surrounding pavement. If a patch fails in a year or two, the process was wrong, not the concept.

### Can potholes be repaired in winter?

Temporarily, yes, cold patch works in any weather and we use it for emergency fills. Permanent hot mix repairs need the plants open and reasonable temperatures, which in Lancaster County generally means April through November. If you call us in January, we’ll stabilize the hole and schedule the real fix for spring.

### Do more potholes mean I need to repave?

Not necessarily. It’s about the ratio of failed area to healthy pavement, and whether the base underneath is still solid. A few holes in firm asphalt: patch. Widespread alligator cracking with holes opening everywhere: it’s time to talk resurfacing. We’ll give you a straight answer either way, see our patch-vs-repave guide linked above.
